# Background

This is a postmortem for The Camera that Bleeds (TCTB). What started as a university project between me and my friend Davis Snider grew over the course of several years to attract nearly a dozen talented artists, audio folks & others who poured their hearts & creative energy into the game with the hopes it could become something great. To make a long story short, after several years of working on demos & pitching to publishers, we failed to get it off the ground and decided to shelve it indefinitely. # Timeline

SPRING 2019

TCTB started as a little "game jam" game that Davis and I decided to put together over a weekend in February. We were fairly new friends, and both of us happened to be on boring internships and were looking for things to do. This was a concept that I had talked about with him before and we were both compelled by. "What if there were ghosts you could only see in photos?" Looking back at even very early gameplay, I'm still compelled by it! It's a great concept.

![test image]({{ site.url | absolute_path}}/assets/images/TCTB/TCTB_FirstPrototype.mp4)

Davis says the game jam was his way of "forcing me to make a game with him", something I was happy to do. At the time, I was working on <a href="/2016/08/22/exalt.html">Exalt</a>, a solo project, but working alone is lonely & I was very excited about this concept.

FALL 2019

We learned about a course at RIT called Production Studio that allows students to work on a project of their choosing together over the semester, and saw this as an opportunity to carve out more time to work on our project and earn course credit for it. This ended up being a class we took twice in a row with the same professor, Aaron Nieboer. Aaron is an industry veteran, indie studio founder and art director, and was instrumental in getting TCTB off the ground in its early stages.

![test image]({{ site.url | absolute_path}}/assets/images/TCTB/Oct2019_Screenshot1.png)
![test image]({{ site.url | absolute_path}}/assets/images/TCTB/Oct2019_Screenshot2.png)
![test image]({{ site.url | absolute_path}}/assets/images/TCTB/Oct2019_Screenshot3.png)

 Early in that fall semester, we brought TCTB to a project networking event at RIT where teams looking for collaborators pitched their projects to artists looking for work. We had a lot of interest in our project - most projects there were standard fare for the event - films and other animation stuff, but a lot of folks in the 3D scene are there for games & were very excited about a game project to work on. We were concerned about bringing on too many folks and overwhelming ourselves, so we limited ourselves to 3 additional members - (NAME?), Vic and Miki. These folks were great conributors to the project over that semester, and Vic and Miki would end up sticking around for the entire duration of the project.

![test image]({{ site.url | absolute_path}}/assets/images/TCTB/10_29_operatingtheatre.png)

SPRING 2020

Aaron also taught a project-based 3D art class, and in that second spring semester, he matchmade art students in that class with designer-engineer teams in Production Studio. This cross-disciplinary collaboration was something that's surprisingly uncommon during RIT's Game Design & Development curriculum (and as I understand, it's 3D Digital Design curriculum as well), but I can't emphasize enough how vital it was for TCTB's success and our collective growth as game developers. At least for me, it was an incredible opportunity to learn how to organize a larger team, communicate with people from other technical & creative skillsets, understand the difficulties of scaling up team sizes, provide clear and actionable feedback and structure an asset production pipeline. It also forced us to address a longstanding hole in the game - our aesthetic.

![test image]({{ site.url | absolute_path}}/assets/images/TCTB/keyart.png)

Before spring semester, we had an idea of our aesthetic represented in key art I made (above), but our game was entirely greyscale using Unreal's out-of-the-box greyboxing assets & lighting, with a touch of volumetric lighting. We had a very gameplay-first approach to the game, but with artists coming on board, it was important that we use their time & skill best. Early in this semester I spent most of my development time creating work & an asset production pipeline for our artists & working on our game's aesthetic.

![test image]({{ site.url | absolute_path}}/assets/images/TCTB/1_27_20.png)

From January (above) until early March (below), our game's aesthetic used varying postprocess effects from <a href="https://4loup.itch.io/illustrious">4loup's "illustrious" pack on itch.io</a> - I spent a lot of time iterating by tweaking parameters in their pack, trying to figure out how the postprocess effects worked, what artefacting they were vulnerable to, and generally exploring what aesthetic we were going for (do we want greys? bloom? color?)

![test image]({{ site.url | absolute_path}}/assets/images/TCTB/2_28_20_dearreaders.png)
![test image]({{ site.url | absolute_path}}/assets/images/TCTB/3_7_20_concept.png)

I realized that if we were to get anywhere, I had to write a postprocess effect myself. This is where I fell in love with Unreal's material graph & really discovered my passion for writing shaders & technical art as a discipline. I had an idea for a cel shader that could support three color bands (dark, mid, light) and still stick to the monochrome style we liked so much, if the middle band simply inverted the texture to make it white pen on black background instead of black pen on white background, which generally has the effect of darkening the region but preserving the detail.

# Successes

- Arguably one of the best game projects to come out of RIT
- Really good timing & capitalization off of a growing desire for cross-disciplinary collaboration @ RIT 
- Great portfolio pieces for everyone I think?
- Huge for my growth as a tech artist, art director, manager, designer, etc.

# Failures

- got away from the core with all the mystery stuff? mystery stuff is cool but we lost some of the magic that was present in that first prototype we made.
- should probably have shot lower as far as scope and budget is concerned, especially given our lack of experience shipping games.
